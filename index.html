Case studies

What each project is, who it is for, the decisions behind it, and where it stands today.

AussieBattlers

Problem
Artillery games are fun with friends, but most are built for desktops and don’t suit quick phone sessions or local humour.
Audience
Mates playing on their phones, in short sessions, who like a joke.
Product decisions
Touch-first controls for aiming and firing, custom weapons and chaotic maps, and a fictional-brand edition for public play.
Today
A multiplayer beta, previously played online as Bogans vs Eshays and now moving to the AussieBattlers name.
My contribution
Concept, product direction, control and interaction design, AI-assisted implementation, and iteration from playtest feedback.
Next
Open the beta under the AussieBattlers name and keep tuning controls and reconnection from feedback.

Joshi’s Nightmare

Problem
Make a slicing game that feels immediate and fair on a phone, with a character who reacts to how you play.
Audience
Phone players, with iPhone as the primary target.
Product decisions
Portrait layout, swipe-to-slice with fast-swipe collision, a dose meter that raises the difficulty as mushrooms get through, and a sound bank tuned for low latency.
Today
A playable prototype built with HTML5 canvas and TypeScript, with automated tests and browser smoke tests.
My contribution
Game concept, feel and difficulty design, requirements, AI-assisted build, and hands-on phone testing.
Next
Keep refining difficulty, sound and animation from playtest feedback.

Trackn

Problem
Taste in music is personal, but services make it hard to keep your own picks, say why they matter, or find who really shares them.
Audience
People who care about music and want to connect over it.
Product decisions
The user’s own picks and words are the core, and the product is independent of any one streaming service. Connections are designed to be honest about versions and shared artists and songs.
Today
An interactive demo of the product and its connection reveal, built with Next.js and TypeScript. Sign-up and real profiles are not switched on yet, and there is no public release.
My contribution
Product concept and direction, requirements, and AI-assisted build with human review of the spec.
Next
Switch on accounts and run a private test with real catalogues.

Jellyfish

Problem
Running a family involves a lot of invisible work: noticing what needs doing, deciding who does it, remembering when, and checking it got done. Often one parent carries it all in their head.
Audience
Families, starting with the parent who carries the mental load.
Product direction
Emma owns the product. Her brief defines the loop: Information → Action → Owner → Time → Reminder → Completion. She has confirmed that Jellyfish must be useful to one person before anyone else joins, that events happen on their own while tasks need someone to act, and that Today is the main everyday screen.
Today
An early prototype: a demo site with an illustrative Today list, an exploratory prototype using a made-up household, and the backend foundations. The working features (sign-in, adding tasks, owners and reminders) are not built yet, and no real family data is stored.
My contribution
Technical setup, the build pipeline and AI-assisted development, working to Emma’s direction. The product thinking, decisions and discovery with families are Emma’s.
Next
Emma is defining the smallest first test with a few families. The first build step is a working Today screen.

About

I’ve worked across customer success, product, operations and implementations, including at foundU. That means I’ve sat close to real customer problems and to the teams who fix them.

Now I use that to build. I prototype quickly with AI coding tools such as Claude Code, but the product judgment is mine: what to build, what to cut, how it should feel, and whether it actually works when I test it on a phone.

Outside work I’m into music and start-ups, and I like jokes that land.
